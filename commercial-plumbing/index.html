How we handle business calls

We ask about the type of space, the fixtures affected and whether you own or lease, since the landlord may be responsible for some systems. A quick phone call decides whether to send a drain crew, a repair plumber or both. For grease and recurring blockages, we recommend hydro jetting on a schedule instead of repeated emergency calls.

We coordinate around your business hours. Many commercial jobs are done early in the morning or after closing to keep you open.

Planning around business hours

A plumbing failure rarely respects your schedule, but the repair can. When a restaurant closes at 10 p.m., an after-hours crew can clear a drain or swap a heater element before the morning prep. For retail and offices, early morning visits keep customers out of the way. Tell the dispatcher your hours and any constraints, such as health inspections, deliveries or tenants sharing the same lines.

We document what we find and what we do, so you have a record for the landlord, the insurer or a health inspector. That record also helps you spot patterns, such as a drain that backs up every few weeks or a water heater that fails every few years.

Preventive habits that cut emergency calls

Grease interceptors and kitchen drains need regular attention. Floor drains in back rooms need to be flushed and the traps kept wet. Restroom fixtures should be checked on a schedule. Water heaters in busy kitchens benefit from flushing and anode rod checks. Staff training matters too, since a single bad habit, such as pouring grease down a drain, causes the call you pay for later.

  • Keep a list of shutoff locations for the building and each tenant space
  • Post the 24/7 number where staff can see it
  • Schedule jetting for grease-prone lines before they back up
  • Test and log backflow devices where required
  • Replace worn supply lines and angle stops during slow periods

Safety steps and codes for businesses

Commercial plumbing follows the State Plumbing Code, which adopts the International Plumbing Code (IPC), and food-service drains and grease control are also subject to local health and utility rules. Here are the safety steps we ask staff to take before we arrive.

  • Keep customers and staff away from wet floors and electrical equipment
  • Shut off the water to the affected area or the building main
  • Do not use chemical drain cleaners in a full line
  • Photograph damage and note the time
  • Tell us if a health inspection or a delivery is waiting

Leaks, backflow and planned testing

Businesses have leak problems that homes do not. Shared walls, flat roofs, long runs in ceilings and several tenants on one meter make a hidden leak harder to trace and harder to assign. We use the same listening, thermal and pressure methods we use in homes, and we write up what we find so you can discuss it with the landlord.

Some leak work for businesses ends without a repair order, because the leak sits on a part of the building the owner controls, and we say so honestly.

Backflow prevention devices protect the public water supply from contamination, and many commercial buildings are required to have them tested on a schedule. It is planned work, not an emergency. Ask your water utility what applies to your property, and keep the test records. If a device fails a test, it needs repair or replacement and a retest before it is accepted.
